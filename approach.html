My Approach

How the model works

Five steps take a microscope photo to a highlighted result. Each step is explained in plain words, with the technical name underneath.

Why a graph?

A trained microscopist does not judge one cell on its own: they look at how it relates to the cells around it, for example how parasites cluster [3]. Graph attention networks can learn this kind of relationship, and they have worked well in other medical images [9], [10]. Using them for malaria is the new idea in this research.

The five steps in more detail

1

Find the spots

A simple stain filter finds every dark-stained spot in the image that could be a parasite. It needs no labels, so it works the same way in a clinic.

Label-free candidate detection
2

Connect neighbours

Each spot becomes a point in a graph, described by its shape, colour and a small picture of it, and is linked to its 5 nearest neighbours.

k-nearest-neighbour graph, k = 5
3

Learn what matters

A graph attention network looks at each spot together with its neighbours and learns which neighbours matter most for deciding: parasite or not [9].

Graph attention network, 8 heads
4

Make it smaller

A larger ‘teacher’ model trains a small ‘student’ model to copy its answers. The proposal’s target was a student that keeps at least 90% of the teacher’s accuracy score (AUC-ROC) [12].

Knowledge distillation
5

Run on a phone

The student model is exported to ONNX, a file of under 1 MB, and runs offline in an Android app on an ordinary phone with about 4 GB of RAM. The app circles the likely parasites and shows a count.

ONNX Runtime on Android

Why a smaller model?

Earlier Makerere work found that the most accurate model (Faster R-CNN) was too heavy for phones, while the lighter phone model (SSD MobileNet) missed more parasites [2], [3]. This research tests whether a distilled graph model can be both accurate and light.
